For most of history, nobody had any particular reason to believe the stars were made of anything in
particular — they were simply lights in the sky, and Earth's own chemistry was the only chemistry
anyone knew. Spectroscopy changed that at a stroke: point a prism at a star, and its light carries
the same fingerprints iron, sodium and hydrogen leave in a laboratory flame.

## What this claim actually asks

The Orion Nebula and the Helix Nebula could not be more different in origin — one is a stellar
nursery, the other a dying star's own shell — and yet both carry the identical H-alpha line,
hydrogen's own red glow. Finding that one line in two completely unrelated objects is the actual
historical proof that the universe runs on one shared chemistry, not a different recipe for every
star. See [[sky/object_orion_nebula]] and [[sky/object_helix_nebula]].

## The stages

A plate is only a photograph until its lines are actually read — the first stage asks you to decode
both objects; [[spectroscopy/lines]] explains the verb. The second asks you to own real ground
filters for the two lines this pairing rests on, so you can find them again on anything else that
carries them. The third spends two exposed plates to file the claim for good, the same way a real
published result spends its own evidence.
