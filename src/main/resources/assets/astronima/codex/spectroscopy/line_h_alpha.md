656.3 nm — hydrogen's Balmer-alpha transition, and the reason nearly every nebula photograph you
have ever seen carries the same red-pink cast. [[spectroscopy/lines]] explains what a spectral line
actually is; this page is about this one specifically.

## Where it shows up here

The Orion Nebula and the Helix Nebula both carry it, from opposite ends of a star's life — a young
nursery and a dying core's own shell. Andromeda's disc carries a trace of it too, from its own
ongoing star formation; its companion M32 carries none at all, having no cold gas left to ionise.
Finding the identical line across such different sources is the real evidence behind
[[research/claim_same_elements]].

## Reading it on a real spectrum

A real peak never sits exactly at 656.3 nm on a captured plate — every object's own motion shifts it
by a small, real amount, different for every target. Matching the shape of the peak first, then
checking it lands near this line's own rest wavelength once that shift is allowed for, is the actual
skill the decode verb is asking for.
