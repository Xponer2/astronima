Nothing out here breaks in your hand. That is not a convenience — it is load-bearing: a tool that
could vanish at the wrong moment could strand a player with no way back to metal at all, and this
mod does not build traps it cannot get you out of. So a tool does not fail by running out of a
hidden number. It **wears**, the way a real edge does, and there is a floor under how bad that gets.

## Archard's equation, not a durability bar

```
V = K · W · L / H
```

Wear volume from a normal load over a sliding distance, against the hardness of the softer
material. The part worth keeping is the shape of it: **wear rate runs inversely to hardness.** A
tool twice as hard does not last a little longer, it lasts twice as long — which is what makes
forging quality govern a tool's *entire working life*, not just how it feels on day one.

![Mining speed against blocks cut. Green: a well-forged head. Red: a head pressed straight from grains with no forging at all. Both are worn on the same clock; only the hardness differs. The dashed line is the floor neither can cross.](astronima:textures/codex/tool.png 160x100)

Both curves are the same law at two hardnesses. The gap between them is the entire argument for
forging properly rather than pressing and walking away: a pressed head is not a *worse* pickaxe —
it is the same pickaxe on a much shorter clock, because it never got hard.

## The floor is the promise

The dashed line is a fraction of full speed, and **nothing in this game can push a tool below it.**
A spent edge still cuts — slowly, badly, but it moves rock. That single number is the guarantee
that no sequence of bad luck can leave you holding a pickaxe that does nothing: the worst tool in
the game still gets you to more metal.

> This is why the edge display never claims a tool is "broken." SHARP, DULLED, BLUNT, SPENT are
> speeds, not a countdown to zero.

## Chipping is a different failure, and it is sudden

Work a piece past its ductility limit at the forge and it comes off **cracked** — see
[[ore/processing]] for the forge itself. A cracked head does not wear worse; it holds an edge
normally and then, the moment its edge condition crosses into the next band, loses a chunk of it
all at once. Real work-hardened metal does exactly this: it holds beautifully right up to the point
it does not. A cracked tool costs you a resharpening early, and there is no way to un-crack it —
which is the price of a fast forging job, paid later rather than at the anvil.

## Sharpening spends the tool's own waste

Grind the edge back on **packed tailings** — the fine silicate the magnet did not want. That is
genuinely what a whetstone is: bonded fine abrasive. The waste left over from making metal is what
maintains the tools made from it, which is a real relationship between two systems rather than a
recipe convenience.

Three grinds to a well-forged head, one to a pressed one — there is less body in a pressed head to
grind back in the first place. A head with none left is not refused; it keeps cutting at whatever
edge it has, same as always.

Where the metal itself comes from is [[ore/processing]]; what forging trades against ductility is
the cold forge section of that page.
