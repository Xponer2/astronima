You are already wearing it, and it is already broken. Every subsystem came out of the wreck faulty,
and getting them working again *is* the opening of this game — not a tutorial in front of it.

> A suit is not armour with a number. It is seven separate machines strapped to a person, and each
> one of them fails in its own way and takes its own job with it.

## What each part actually does

| Part | While it is broken | Fix it with |
|---|---|---|
| **Helmet seal** | the suit cannot hold pressure at all — nothing else matters | sealant patch |
| **Tank mount** | no oxygen tank can be fitted | latch set |
| **Regulator** | your tank drains twice as fast | calibrated valve |
| **Scrubber bay** | exhaled CO₂ collects inside your helmet | LiOH cartridge |
| **Thermal layer** | no protection from cold or heat | insulation weave |
| **Status display** | the suit tells you nothing about itself | salvaged circuit |
| **Sealed gloves** | anything you handle goes straight onto your skin | insulation weave |

Fix the helmet seal first. It is the one that makes every other repair pointless while it is open,
and it is also the cheapest.

## Repair is a procedure, not a progress bar

Hold the suit in one hand and the part in the other. Each subsystem opens **its own job**, because
different physical work needs different actions — you do not solder a board the way you torque a
latch:

- **Helmet seal** — lay sealant along the crack and stop in the green band.
- **Tank mount** — torque each latch in turn, and feel for where it seats.
- **Regulator** — turn the dial until the error needle sits on nothing, and hold it there.
- **Scrubber bay** — one committed push. Feel the detent, release on it. Push past it and you crush
  the gasket.
- **Thermal layer** — follow the channel with the mouse without wandering off it.
- **Status display** — click each pad. Near misses bridge, and a bridge has to be wicked clean.
- **Sealed gloves** — the odd one out: **find the hole before you patch it.** See below.

Two rules bind all of them. **Failure costs time, never the part** — a player who keeps trying
always finishes. And **how well you did it decides how long it lasts**, so a scrappy job is not
free, it is a job you will be doing again sooner.

## The glove hunt

A pinhole is invisible. You cannot see it, and the game does not draw it — drawing it would leave
nothing to do. So you do what a technician does: inflate the glove, run it under a bead of soap
solution, and listen.

The bubbling **rises as you approach the leak and falls as you pass it**. Follow it to its loudest
point, then patch.

> **Do not patch at the first sound you hear.** A patch laid over the edge of a hole holds on the
> bench and splits the first time you grip something. The panel will tell you afterwards that you
> patched in the wrong place, and you will get to look again.

The glove bleeds down while you hunt, so the signal you are searching by fades as you use it.
Re-inflate and carry on. Nothing here can strand you; it only costs minutes.

## It wears out

Every part has a lifetime measured in **the stress that wears that particular part**, not in
wall-clock time. The seal counts sealed hours. The regulator counts gas actually flowing through
it. The thermal layer counts time somewhere that is trying to kill you.

Gloves go first, at about two hours of handling, because gloves are the part you actually use.

A suit on a hook is not wearing out.

## Before you go outside

- **Pre-breathe.** Pure oxygen for a while washes dissolved nitrogen out of your tissue. Skip it
  and a fast decompression will give you the bends — see [[life/airlock]].
- **Check the display.** If the status display is dark you are flying blind, which is survivable
  and stupid.
- **Take a spare cartridge.** The scrubber is the consumable that runs out mid-EVA.

The gloves matter for something other than temperature now: see [[medical/clean]].
