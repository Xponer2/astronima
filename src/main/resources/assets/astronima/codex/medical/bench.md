You cannot treat an infection you have not identified, and you cannot identify one by looking at
the patient. Everything below is what a real diagnostic laboratory does, in the order it does it.

> The plate is the notebook. Every test you run is written on the dish, so what one bench finds
> out, the next bench receives.

## 1. Streak, and streak properly

Drag the sample across the agar in four quadrants, each pass starting from the tail of the last.
The point is **dilution**: by the fourth quadrant single cells are landing far enough apart to grow
into separate colonies. Smear it on in one go and you get a lawn you cannot pick anything out of.

![Four quadrants, each starting from the tail of the last. By the fourth, single colonies.](astronima:textures/codex/streak.png 120x120)

## 2. Incubate at 37 °C for 18 hours

- **Below 20 °C** nothing grows at all.
- **Fastest at blood heat**, slower either side of it.
- **Above 47 °C you have cooked it** — and a cooked plate looks exactly like a plate that was
  sterile. Nothing on it tells you which. That is why a laboratory owns a thermometer.

## 3. The Gram stain

Crystal violet, then iodine, then **alcohol**, then safranin. The iodine locks the violet into a
complex too big to wash out of a thick cell wall; the alcohol strips it from a thin one. So a
**Gram-positive stays purple** and a **Gram-negative goes pink** from the counterstain.

> Leave the alcohol on too long and even a thick wall gives up the dye. Your Gram-positive reads
> pink, you take the wrong branch, you pick from the wrong drug list, and you breed resistance to
> something that was never going to work.

The only warning you get is a contradiction: **Gram-negative cocci in clusters is not an organism.**
If the microscope and the slide disagree, the slide is wrong.

![Only the alcohol column differs between a thick wall and a thin one. That is the test, and that is the step with the clock on it.](astronima:textures/codex/gram.png 160x74)

## 4. Look at it

Shape and arrangement. Cocci or bacilli; single, pairs, chains or clusters. It is where half of them
get their names — *staphylē* is a bunch of grapes, *streptos* is twisted like a chain.

## 5. One drop of peroxide

Catalase splits hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen, and it fizzes. It is the first test any
student learns, because it separates the two commonest Gram-positive cocci in about a second.

## 6. Kirby–Bauer, and a ruler

Lawn the organism, lay paper discs loaded with a known amount of drug, incubate, and **measure the
clear ring in millimetres**.

The drug diffuses out from the disc down a concentration gradient. The organism grows wherever the
local concentration is below the amount needed to stop it, and does not grow where it is above — so
**the edge of the ring is that concentration**, and measuring a distance tells you a dose. A more
susceptible organism is stopped further out.

Compare the diameter against the published breakpoints: susceptible, intermediate, resistant.
Intermediate is a real answer, not a shrug — it means the drug will work at a higher dose or
somewhere it concentrates.

> **Swab the lawn too thickly and every ring closes in.** The plate reads resistant to everything,
> is perfectly self-consistent, and is completely wrong. Real laboratories standardise the
> inoculum against a turbidity standard for exactly this reason.

![Three discs, three rings. Wide is susceptible, none is resistant, and the middle one is a decision.](astronima:textures/codex/zones.png 160x100)

## 7. Then, and only then, treat

Use what the plate said was susceptible. Guessing costs you that drug for ever against this
organism — see [[start/tiers|the ladder]] for where the rest of the medical tier goes.
