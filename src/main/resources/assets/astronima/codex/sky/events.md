The sky here is not scenery. Things happen in it, on their own schedule, and every one of them is
**periodic** — not random. Watch long enough and you can learn when. That is the whole point: a sky
you can predict is a sky you can plan around.

## No shooting stars. Ever.

Whatever else you see up there, you will never see a meteor streak. A shooting star is not the rock
— it is *air* being heated white-hot as the rock ploughs through it. There is no air here, so there
is nothing to glow, and an incoming body arrives entirely unannounced.

What you get instead is the **impact**: a pinpoint flash somewhere out toward the horizon and a slow
plume of regolith afterwards. First warning and arrival are the same event.

## The Sun is not steady

**Flares.** The Sun brightens, its corona swells, and the whole sky gets fractionally brighter with
it. Look at the disc when it is quiet and you will find darker patches on it — sunspots, where the
Sun's own magnetic field has punched through the surface and exposed cooler material below. The
loops arching off the edge are the same field made visible, hot gas following the field lines out
and back down. More spots means more loops means more flares; they are all one phenomenon.

**Transits.** Something crosses in front of the Sun and part of it goes dark for a while. Not a
total blackout — a bite taken out, and then it passes.

That one is not just a view. **A transit genuinely dims what reaches the ground**, and anything of
yours that lives on sunlight will feel it. If a solar array or a retort stalls for no reason you can
find, look up before you take it apart.

## The slow ones

**A passing body.** Another rock in this belt, close enough to see, crossing the sky in a couple of
seconds and tumbling as it goes. Small bodies are not spheres and do not hold still — they turn
end over end, which is why it flickers as it passes.

**A comet.** This one arrives over days, holds for days more, and takes weeks to fade — and it
*moves*, drifting a real distance against the fixed stars over its visit. Its head glows faintly
green from carbon vapour boiling off it; its tail runs blue where the gas is ionised and warmer
where the tail is sunlit dust.

> **An honest note about that tail.** In reality a comet's tail is *not* a wake — nothing is left
> behind it. The solar wind blows the tail radially away from the Sun no matter which way the comet
> is travelling, which means a real comet heading away from the Sun has its tail running **ahead**
> of it. It is one of the most commonly-drawn-wrong things in astronomy. The comet you will see here
> is drawn the wrong way round on purpose — trailing behind its motion — because the correct version
> reads to the eye as though the thing were flying backwards. Now you know which one is real.

**A supernova.** A star that was not there yesterday, brightening over days into something brighter
than anything else up there, then fading over months. It will not come back. Whatever you were going
to do about it, do it while it is up.

## The one you can actually study

A supernova is the only thing in this sky whose **spectrum changes while you watch it**. Everything
else — the Sun, a nebula, a galaxy — reads the same today as it will next year. This one does not,
because it is genuinely still happening:

| Phase | What the instrument reads |
|---|---|
| First days | Ferociously hot, tens of thousands of kelvin, and almost featureless. Too hot and too violent for any line to stand out |
| The long plateau | Cooled to roughly the Sun's own temperature. Hydrogen, helium and calcium lines appear as the debris recombines |
| The long decline | Cooler still, and thin enough at last for the **forbidden line** to come through — the same [O III] that needs a better vacuum than any laboratory can make |

That last row is worth waiting for. It is the one line in the catalogue that will not show up on
anything ordinary, and a dying star throwing off its own outer layers is exactly the kind of place
it belongs. See [[spectroscopy/lines|Every light has a fingerprint]] for what that line is and why it
took sixty years to explain.

Point {item:astronima:spectrograph} at it more than once, days apart. Two plates of the same
supernova will not match, and that is not an instrument fault — that is the star changing.
